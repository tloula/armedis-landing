Riding alone is one of the best parts of cycling. You pick the route, set the pace, and turn around whenever you feel like it. No waiting at the top of a climb, no group chat debate about the start time. Just you, the road, and however many miles you have in your legs that day.

But solo rides also have a quiet downside. When you ride with a group, someone notices if you drop off the back. When you ride alone, the only people who know you're out are the ones you told, and they might not think twice until hours later.

A **check-in service for solo cyclists** closes that gap. You start a timer before you clip in, check back in when you're home, and if you don't, the people you choose are told automatically.

---

## Why Solo Rides Can Go Unnoticed

Most solo cyclists are careful. They wear a helmet, carry a spare tube, and ride routes they know. The risk isn't recklessness. It's that a long ride naturally creates long stretches where nobody expects to hear from you.

- **Rides are long by design.** A two- or three-hour ride is normal, so a late return doesn't stand out right away.
- **Quiet roads are the appeal.** The back roads and gravel routes that make solo riding great are often the ones with the least traffic.
- **Your phone is in a pocket.** You might not hear a call or see a text until you stop, so missed messages look normal to people at home.
- **Plans change mid-ride.** A detour, an extra loop, or a café stop can stretch a ride well past what you said.
- **Small problems can turn into long delays.** A flat in a dead zone, a mechanical, or a bonk can leave you walking a long way.

None of this is a reason to stop riding alone. It's a reason to set up a simple safety net that expects you back and speaks up when you're not.

### Safety Timers for Every Ride

A safety timer is built for exactly the kind of thing a solo ride is: a one-off stretch of time where you want someone to know if you don't come back.

Before you head out, set a duration and an optional note, like "2.5 hour road ride, river loop" or "gravel ride, back by noon." When you're home, check back in. If the timer runs out and you haven't, there's a five-minute grace period, and then your contacts are notified.

That grace period matters on a bike. You might be rolling into your driveway right as the timer ends. Five minutes gives you room to unclip, put the bike away, and tap "I'm back" without setting off a false alarm.

## Tips for Using a Check-In Service on Solo Rides

- **Set the timer for your realistic finish, plus a buffer.** Headwinds, flats, and café stops happen. Give yourself some slack so alerts stay meaningful.
- **Use the note field.** "River loop, out-and-back" tells your contact far more than a time alone.
- **Extend instead of ignoring.** If the ride is going long, a quick "need 30 more minutes" text keeps your contacts from worrying.
- **Pick contacts who can act.** Choose people who would actually call you, drive your usual route, or know who to call.
- **Talk through the plan.** Let your contacts know what an alert means and what they should try first.
- **Check in as soon as you're home.** Make it part of your post-ride routine, right next to refilling your bottles.
