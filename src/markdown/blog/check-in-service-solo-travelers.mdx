Traveling alone is one of the best things you can do for yourself. You set the pace. You eat where you want, wander where you want, and change plans on a whim. Nobody is waiting on you.

That last part is also the catch. When no one is waiting on you, no one notices when something goes wrong. A missed train, a dead phone, a twisted ankle on a quiet trail, a ride that didn't go where it was supposed to. Back home, your family assumes you're busy having fun. Days can pass before anyone thinks to ask.

A **check-in service for solo travelers** fixes that without taking any of the freedom away. You keep traveling the way you like. If you go quiet when you shouldn't, the people you trust find out automatically.

---

## Why Solo Travelers Need a Safety Net

Most solo travelers have some version of an informal system. You post a few photos. You text your mom when you land. You tell a friend the name of your hostel.

The problem is that informal systems depend on someone noticing an absence. And absences are easy to explain away. "She's probably on a bus with no signal." "He said he might go off-grid for a few days." Everyone's reasoning is sensible, and that is exactly why nothing happens.

Travel also strips away the routines that would normally catch a problem at home. There's no coworker expecting you on Monday, no neighbor who sees your car hasn't moved. You're surrounded by strangers, and strangers don't know your plans.

A check-in service replaces "someone would probably notice" with a clear rule: if you don't check in, your contacts are told.

### Safety Timers for the Riskier Moments

Some parts of a trip deserve a closer eye than a once-a-day check-in. A late arrival in a new city. A solo day hike. Meeting someone you connected with online. A night out on your own.

That's what safety timers are for. Before you head out, set a duration and an optional note, like "walking from the station to the hostel, 40 minutes" or "day hike, back by 4pm." When you're done, you check back in. If the timer runs out and you haven't, there's a five-minute grace period, and then your contacts are notified.

Your note goes a long way here. Instead of "something might be wrong," your family knows roughly what you were doing and when you expected to be done.

You can also create a timer by texting CheckIn More's number in plain language, with no app required. Text "need 30 more minutes" to extend it, or "I'm back" when you're safe. That's handy when you're juggling a backpack, a map, and a phone at 2% battery.

## Freedom Without Being Tracked

A lot of travel safety advice boils down to "share your location with someone." That works for some people. For others it feels like a leash, and it creates its own anxiety: a family member watching a dot that hasn't moved in an hour, wondering whether to panic.

CheckIn More is not a GPS tracker. There's no live location sharing and nothing running in the background watching where you go. You decide when to check in and when to start a timer. Your contacts don't see your every move; they hear from the app only if you miss a check-in or ask for help.

That's a much better deal for both sides. You get to be fully present on your trip. Your family gets a clear signal they can trust, instead of a map they have to interpret.

## Tips for Setting Up Before You Leave

- **Pick a check-in time that fits your travel days.** Late morning is often easier than early morning when you're changing cities or sleeping in after a night train.
- **Give yourself a realistic window.** Bus rides, flights, and patchy signal happen. A longer window cuts down on false alarms.
- **Add more than one contact.** Someone in your home time zone and someone awake when you are is a good combination.
- **Tell your contacts what an alert means.** Agree ahead of time on what they should do first, like calling you, then your accommodation.
- **Use timers for the in-between moments.** Arrivals, solo excursions, and nights out are where a timer earns its keep.
