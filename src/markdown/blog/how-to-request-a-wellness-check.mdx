You've called twice. You've texted. It's been a day, maybe two, and the person who always answers hasn't. Maybe it's your dad who lives alone a few states away, a friend who just moved to a new city, or a neighbor whose mail has started piling up.

At some point the question shifts from "should I be worried?" to "what do I actually do?" For a lot of people, the answer is a wellness check.

This guide walks through **how to request a wellness check**, what to say when you call, what usually happens next, and how a simple daily check-in routine can help you find out sooner, often without needing one at all.

---

## What Is a Wellness Check?

A wellness check (sometimes called a welfare check) is when someone, usually a local police officer or sheriff's deputy, goes to a person's home to make sure they're okay. It's typically requested by a family member, friend, neighbor, or landlord who can't reach the person and has a reason to be concerned.

The officer knocks, tries to make contact, and checks whether the person is safe. If they answer the door and everything is fine, that's usually the end of it. If there's no answer and there are signs something is wrong, the responder decides what to do next.

Procedures vary from place to place, so treat the steps below as a general guide rather than a rulebook for your area.

---

## When It Makes Sense to Ask for One

A wellness check is meant for genuine concern, not for a missed text after lunch. It's worth considering when:

- **Someone has gone unusually quiet.** They normally answer within hours, and now it's been much longer.
- **There's a health factor.** They have a condition, take medication, or have recently been ill or had a fall.
- **Something seems off.** A worrying message, a sudden change in routine, or a neighbor noticing lights left on or mail piling up.
- **You can't get there yourself.** You live too far away, and there's no one nearby you can send.

If you believe someone is in immediate danger, call 911 (or your local emergency number). That's an emergency, not a wellness check.

## How to Request a Wellness Check, Step by Step

### 1. Try the People Closest First

Before you call the police, try anyone who can get there faster or more informally. A neighbor, a nearby relative, a building manager, or a friend with a spare key can often knock on the door within minutes. Sometimes that's all it takes to learn their phone died or they've been napping.

### 2. Find the Right Number

For a non-urgent wellness check, call the **non-emergency line** for the police or sheriff's department where the person lives, not where you live. A quick search for the city or county name plus "police non-emergency number" usually turns it up. If you can't find it, 911 dispatchers can generally route you.

### 3. Have the Details Ready

Dispatchers can help faster when you can tell them:

- The person's full name, age, and home address (including apartment or unit number)
- Your relationship to them and how to reach you
- When you last heard from them and how you tried to contact them
- Why you're concerned: health conditions, medications, recent events, anything out of character
- Whether they live alone, have pets, or own a vehicle (and what it looks like)
- Anything that helps responders get in, like a gate code or who has a key

### 4. Stay Reachable

Keep your phone on and nearby. The responding officer or dispatch may call you back with questions or to let you know what they found. If you hear from your loved one in the meantime, call back and let dispatch know so they can cancel or update the request.

## What Usually Happens Next

An officer goes to the address and tries to make contact. If the person answers and is fine, the officer will typically let them know someone was worried and may pass along a message.

If no one answers, the responder looks for signs of trouble and decides whether further action is needed. How that plays out depends on local policy and what they find. You'll usually get a call back either way, though timing depends on how busy things are.

It's worth knowing that a wellness check can feel like a lot for the person on the other end. A knock from an officer is jarring, especially for someone who was simply asleep or ignoring their phone. That's not a reason to hold back when you're truly worried, but it is a reason to have a gentler system in place for everyday reassurance. ## The Hard Part: How Long Before You Know

The biggest problem with wellness checks isn't the process. It's the gap before anyone realizes one is needed.

For people who live alone, that gap can be long. Nobody expects a call every day. A quiet weekend looks normal. By the time someone thinks, "Wait, when did I last hear from Mom?", a lot of time may have passed.

What actually shortens that gap is a routine that expects to hear from someone, and speaks up quickly when it doesn't.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Who do I call to request a wellness check?**
For non-urgent concerns, call the police or sheriff's non-emergency line in the area where the person lives. If you think they're in immediate danger, call 911 or your local emergency number.

**Can I request a wellness check for someone in another state?**
Generally, yes. Call the non-emergency line for the department that covers their address, not yours, and have their details ready.

**What should I do if I hear from them after I've called?**
Call the same number back and let dispatch know, so they can update or cancel the request.
